## 1. The seven live decks, rewritten

### 1.1 Three-Act Structure (The Fiction Room) · deal question unchanged: "What are you writing?" Novel (9) / Novella (8: drops Midpoint Reversal) / Short story (6: drops First Threshold, Midpoint Reversal, Darkest Point)

| # | Title (unchanged) | Question (the card's placeholder) | "?" in one sentence | Example (*A Christmas Carol*) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hook | What is the first thing that makes this world feel off? | The hook is the first image or line that makes a reader need the next one. | The book opens by insisting a dead man is dead, then shows Scrooge refusing to warm his own office. |
| 2 | Inciting Incident | What happens that the ordinary world can't absorb? | The inciting incident is the event that knocks the ordinary life off balance and starts the story. | Marley's ghost drags his chains into Scrooge's bedroom and promises three more visitors. |
| 3 | First Threshold | What does your protagonist do that they can't take back? | The first threshold is the choice or push that commits the protagonist to the journey. | Scrooge takes the first spirit's hand and is carried out through the wall into his own past. |
| 4 | Rising Complications | What keeps getting harder, and why can't they walk away? | Rising complications are the pressures that grow in the middle so the protagonist can't simply quit. | The past shows him the boy left alone at school and the woman who released him from their engagement. |
| 5 | Midpoint Reversal | What do they learn here that changes what they're after? | The midpoint reversal flips what the protagonist believed, so the second half pursues a different goal. | The present spirit shows him the Cratchits' thin table and throws his own "surplus population" back at him. |
| 6 | Darkest Point | What does defeat look like for them, exactly? | The darkest point is the lowest moment, where losing becomes real and specific. | The last spirit shows a neglected grave with his own name cut into it. |
| 7 | Climax | What is actually being decided in the final confrontation? | The climax is the last confrontation, where the story's real question is settled. | Scrooge grips the spirit's robe and swears he will honour Christmas and live in the past, present and future. |
| 8 | Resolution | What settles, and what is different now? | The resolution shows the world after the climax and what the change costs or gives. | He wakes on Christmas morning, sends the prize turkey to the Cratchits and raises Bob's salary. |
| 9 | Final Image | How does the last image answer the first one? | The final image mirrors the hook so the reader can measure the change. | The man who refused to warm his office becomes a second father to Tiny Tim. |

### 1.2 Feature Screenplay → the Fifteen Beats (The Screen Room) · deal question becomes "What is this for?" Feature / Pilot (note unchanged: "TV Pilot decks are coming…") / **Novel (new: deals the same fifteen as prose beats; the screen words below in brackets soften for prose)**

| # | Title (unchanged) | Question | "?" in one sentence | Example (*The Wonderful Wizard of Oz*, the book) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Opening Image | What does your protagonist's life look like before anything changes? | The opening image is the before-picture the ending will be measured against. | Dorothy on a gray Kansas prairie where even the grass and the house have been bleached by the sun. |
| 2 | Theme Stated | What does someone say, almost in passing, that this story is really about? | The theme stated is the story's question, spoken early by someone who doesn't know it's the theme. | Dorothy tells the Scarecrow that people would rather live in a dreary home than in any beautiful country. |
| 3 | Set-Up | What is missing from this life, and who else lives in it? | The set-up shows the world, the people and the flaw or lack the story will test. | The farm, Uncle Henry and Aunt Em who never laugh, and Toto, the one thing that makes Dorothy laugh. |
| 4 | Catalyst | What happens that there's no going back from? | The catalyst is the event that knocks the protagonist out of the before-picture for good. | The cyclone lifts the farmhouse and sets it down in Oz, on top of a wicked witch. |
| 5 | Debate | What makes your protagonist hesitate before committing? | The debate is the last pause, where the protagonist weighs whether to go at all. | Dorothy asks the Munchkins and the Witch of the North how to get home, and learns the only hope is a wizard far away. |
| 6 | Break into Two | What choice takes them into a world that works by different rules? | Break into two is the step out of the old world and into the story's upside-down version of it. | She puts on the silver shoes, locks the farmhouse door and sets off on the road of yellow brick. |
| 7 | B Story | Which relationship carries what this story is about? | The B story is the second thread, usually a relationship, that carries the theme while the plot runs. | The Scarecrow, the Tin Woodman and the Lion, each missing the thing he most prizes. |
| 8 | Fun and Games | What are the scenes the reader came for [the trailer moments]? | Fun and games delivers the promise of the premise: the scenes the idea exists for. | Kalidahs, a river crossing, a field of deadly poppies and a stork that rescues the Scarecrow. |
| 9 | Midpoint | What false victory or false defeat raises the stakes and starts the clock? | The midpoint is a win or loss that turns out to be the opposite, and the stakes go up. | They reach the Emerald City, and the Wizard tells them they'll get nothing until the Wicked Witch of the West is dead. |
| 10 | Bad Guys Close In | What tightens from outside, and what tightens from inside? | Bad guys close in is the stretch where outer pressure and inner doubt grow together. | The Witch sends wolves, crows, bees and Winkies, then the Winged Monkeys. |
| 11 | All Is Lost | What dies here, literally or in spirit? | All is lost is the lowest point, often marked by a death or something like one. | The Monkeys scatter the Scarecrow's straw, dent the Tin Woodman and carry Dorothy off to be the Witch's slave. |
| 12 | Dark Night of the Soul | What does your protagonist realise, alone, before finding the way through? | The dark night of the soul is the hopeless pause before the protagonist finds what they didn't know they had. | Dorothy, a captive, weeps for home and doesn't know the shoes she has worn all along could carry her there. |
| 13 | Break into Three | What idea arrives from the two stories combining? | Break into three is the solution that comes when the plot and the relationship thread meet. | In a flash of anger over a stolen shoe, Dorothy throws a bucket of water and the Witch melts. |
| 14 | Finale | How does your protagonist prove they've changed by winning their own way? | The finale is the final test, passed in a way the old self couldn't have managed. | The Wizard is unmasked as a humbug, his balloon leaves without her, and she walks on to Glinda. |
| 15 | Final Image | What does the after-picture show that the opening couldn't? | The final image answers the opening image with visible proof of the change. | Dorothy clicks the silver heels together and is rolling in the Kansas grass, running to Aunt Em. |

| # | Title (unchanged) | Question | "?" in one sentence | Example (*Pride and Prejudice*) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Want vs. Need | What do they think they want, and what do they actually need? | Want vs. need is the gap between the goal a character chases and the change they require. | Elizabeth wants to be right about people; she needs to doubt her first readings of them. |

### 1.4 Worldbuilding (The Speculative Annex) · deal question unchanged: "What kind of world?" Fantasy / Science fiction / Other (Rules of the World varies by answer)

| # | Title (unchanged) | Question | "?" in one sentence | Example (*Gulliver's Travels*, Lilliput) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Rules of the World | What can magic do here, and what does it cost? *(fantasy)* · What can the technology do, and what does it cost? *(science fiction)* · What are this world's own rules, and what do they cost? *(other)* | The rules are what is possible in this world and the price of using it; limits make a world believable. | Everything in Lilliput is one-twelfth the size of home, so a man is a mountain and a cow fits in a pocket. |
| 2 | Deep History | What happened long before page one that still shapes everything? | Deep history is the past the characters inherit, whether or not they know it. | A war over which end of an egg to crack has split two nations for generations. |
| 3 | Places & Maps | Where does this story happen, and what does the land demand of the people in it? | Places and maps are the geography that forces choices: distance, borders, weather, routes. | An island kingdom a channel away from its enemy, so every quarrel becomes a naval one. |
| 4 | Cultures & Factions | Who are the groups, and what does each one want? | Cultures and factions are the peoples and parties whose wants collide to make plot. | Two court parties told apart by the height of their heels, each certain the other is ruining the country. |
| 5 | Power & Economy | Who has power, who wants it, and what does it run on? | Power and economy answers who decides and what the world is paid in. | An emperor who rules by spectacle and a treasury that strains to feed one giant guest. |
| 6 | Language Notes | Which words, names and turns of phrase belong only to this world? | Language notes are the sounds of the world: names, invented words, the way people swear and greet. | Gulliver is titled the Man-Mountain in the local tongue, and learns the language in three weeks. |
| 7 | The Iceberg | What do you know that the reader never sees, but that makes the rest true? | The iceberg is the knowledge under the surface that keeps the visible world consistent. | Why the court fears the giant more than it admits, which the narrator never says outright. |

### 1.5 Thesis / Dissertation (The Academy) · deal question unchanged: "Humanities or sciences?" Five foundation cards for both; four chapter cards per answer

| # | Title (unchanged) | Question | "?" in one sentence | Example *(invented: a thesis on how railway timetables changed the Victorian novel's sense of time)* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Research Question | What single question does this whole work exist to answer? | The research question is the one question every chapter serves. | Did the arrival of the public timetable change how novels of the 1850s measured a day? |
| 2 | Methodology | How will you answer the question, and why does this method fit it? | Methodology is the chosen way of answering, justified against the alternatives. | Close reading of time-words in six novels before and after 1848, counted and compared. |
| 3 | Lit-Review Clusters | Which conversations is the existing work already having, and where do you join? | Lit-review clusters group the sources by the argument they share, not by the alphabet. | One cluster on railway history, one on narrative time, one on the serial novel's deadlines. |
| 4 | Evidence Parking Lot | What have you found that doesn't have a home in the argument yet? | The evidence parking lot holds findings, quotations and data until the argument has a place for them. | A letter where a novelist complains that the 9:40 train has ruined his mornings. |
| 5 | Citation Ledger | Which sources will you need to cite, and have you tracked each one? | The citation ledger is the running list of everything that must appear in the bibliography. | Every edition, archive and article used, with the page that will be cited. |
| 6h | Chapter One | What must this chapter establish before the next can build on it? | A first chapter lays the ground: terms, context and the claim in its first form. | The timetable as an object: who printed it, who read it, what it promised. |
| 7h | Chapter Two | Where does the argument deepen or complicate itself? | A second chapter tests the claim against the harder cases. | Two novels that ignore clock time entirely, and why they still prove the point. |
| 8h | Chapter Three | Where does the argument turn toward its own implications? | A third chapter asks what follows if the claim is true. | What a punctual plot does to suspense, and to the reader's patience. |
| 9h | Conclusion | What have you actually shown, and what does it change? | The conclusion states the claim as proven, no larger and no smaller. | The hour replaced the day as the novel's unit, and the serial deadline helped. |
| 6s | Introduction | What gap in the existing work does this study fill? | The introduction names the gap and the question that fills it. | No study has counted time-words across the 1848 divide. |
| 7s | Methods | What exactly did you do, in enough detail to be repeated? | Methods is the recipe another researcher could follow. | Six novels, three time-word categories, two coders, one disagreement rule. |
| 8s | Results | What did you find, reported straight, before any interpretation? | Results is the findings alone, with no argument attached yet. | Clock-time words triple after 1848; day-words fall by a third. |
| 9s | Discussion | What do the results mean, and what don't they mean? | Discussion is where the findings meet the question and their limits are named. | The shift is real, but six novels can't say whether the railway or the serial caused it. |

## 2. The new Tier 1 decks (framework §3a)

### 2.1 Hero's Journey (The Fiction Room) · twelve steps in one sequence · deal question: "What are you writing?" Novel (12) / Short story (8: drops Refusal of the Call, Approach to the Inmost Cave, Reward, The Road Back)

| # | Title | Question | "?" in one sentence | Example (*Treasure Island*) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ordinary World | What does home look like, and what is quietly wrong with it? | The ordinary world is the life the hero will leave, shown so the reader can miss it. | Jim Hawkins at the Admiral Benbow inn, serving a drunken old sea-captain who pays no bill. |
| 2 | Call to Adventure | What arrives that asks the hero to leave? | The call is the invitation or demand that the story cannot proceed without. | The captain dies and Jim finds the oilskin packet with a map of an island and a cross. |
| 3 | Refusal of the Call | Why does the hero hesitate, and what does the hesitation cost? | The refusal is the fear or duty that makes the hero pause, which proves the stakes. | Jim and his mother hide under the bridge while blind Pew's gang ransacks the inn. |
| 4 | Meeting the Mentor | Who gives the hero what they lack, and what is the gift? | The mentor supplies knowledge, a tool or courage; the hero still has to use it. | Dr Livesey and the Squire read the map and fit out a ship; a one-legged cook offers to come along. |
| 5 | Crossing the Threshold | What is the moment of leaving that cannot be undone? | Crossing the threshold is the hero's first step into the story's new world. | The Hispaniola weighs anchor and Jim is at sea for the first time in his life. |
| 6 | Tests, Allies, Enemies | Who turns out to be a friend, who an enemy, and what does the hero learn the hard way? | Tests, allies and enemies is the stretch where the new world teaches its rules. | In the apple barrel Jim overhears Long John Silver planning the mutiny. |
| 7 | Approach to the Inmost Cave | What does the hero prepare for, and what do they fear meeting there? | The approach is the gathering of nerve before the story's central danger. | Jim slips ashore on the island and meets Ben Gunn, the marooned man. |
| 8 | Ordeal | What is the greatest fear faced, and what seems lost in facing it? | The ordeal is the central crisis, where the hero confronts death or its equivalent. | Alone on the drifting Hispaniola, Jim faces Israel Hands and the knife that pins his shoulder. |
| 9 | Reward | What does the hero take from the ordeal, and at what price? | The reward is what the ordeal earns, often with a cost attached. | Jim beaches the ship in a hidden cove: the pirates have lost their only way home. |
| 10 | The Road Back | What drives the hero back toward home, and what pursues them? | The road back turns the story toward the return, with the danger following. | Jim stumbles into the stockade and finds the pirates holding it; Silver keeps him alive. |
| 11 | Resurrection | What final test shows the hero has truly changed? | The resurrection is the last ordeal, passed by the new self rather than the old. | At the empty cache the pirates turn on Silver and Jim, and the friends' ambush saves them. |
| 12 | Return with the Elixir | What does the hero bring back, and what does home look like now? | The return shows the hero home with what the journey won, changed by the winning. | Jim sails home with his share of the treasure, and the island still visits his dreams. |

### 2.3 Scene–Sequel (The Fiction Room) · six steps per scene · deal question: "How many scenes to lay out?" One / Three / Five (one card per step per scene; scenes in columns)

| # | Title | Question | "?" in one sentence | Example *(invented: Marta and the ferry)* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Goal | What does your character want by the end of this scene? | The goal is the concrete thing the character tries to get within the scene. | Marta wants to hand her brother's letter to the ferry captain before the boat leaves. |
| 2 | Conflict | What stands in the way, and how does it fight back? | The conflict is the opposition that makes the goal hard to reach. | A deckhand won't let anyone aboard without a ticket, and the gangway is already lifting. |
| 3 | Disaster | How does the scene end worse than it began? | The disaster is the scene's ending, where the goal is missed or won at a cost. | The ferry pulls out with the letter still in her hand. |
| 4 | Reaction | How does your character feel, in their body, right after? | The reaction is the emotional beat after the disaster, before any thinking. | She sits on a bollard with the letter crushed in her fist, shaking. |
| 5 | Dilemma | What are the bad options now, and why is none of them good? | The dilemma is the choice between poor options that the disaster has left. | Wait a week for the next boat, pay a stranger to chase it, or open the letter herself. |
| 6 | Decision | What do they choose, and what new goal does it set? | The decision is the choice that ends the sequel and becomes the next scene's goal. | She hires a fisherman to run her out to the ferry's first stop. |

### 2.4 Argumentative Essay (The Academy) · seven steps · deal question: "How long?" Short (five: drops Evidence (3) and Counterclaim) / Full (seven)

| # | Title | Question | "?" in one sentence | Example *(invented: closing Main Street to cars on Sunday mornings)* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Claim | What do you believe, in one sentence a reader could disagree with? | The claim is the essay's position, stated so plainly that it can be argued against. | The town should close Main Street to cars every Sunday morning. |
| 2 | Counterclaim | What would the smartest person who disagrees say? | The counterclaim is the best opposing case, taken seriously before it is answered. | Shops on Main Street depend on Sunday drivers who stop on impulse. |
| 3 | Evidence (1) | What is the strongest fact or example for your claim? | Evidence is the fact, example or testimony that makes the claim more than an opinion. | Foot traffic doubled on the two trial Sundays, by the chamber of commerce's own count. |
| 4 | Evidence (2) | What second kind of evidence supports it from another angle? | A second piece of evidence from a different kind of source strengthens the case. | The three cafés on the street reported their best Sunday takings of the year. |
| 5 | Evidence (3) | What evidence answers the counterclaim directly? | Evidence aimed at the counterclaim turns the opposition's point into support. | The hardware store's drivers parked one street over and still came. |
| 6 | Synthesis | How do the pieces fit together into one argument? | Synthesis shows how the evidence and the answered counterclaim add up to the claim. | More people, longer visits and no lost customers: the street works better without the cars. |
| 7 | So What | Why should a reader who doesn't live here care? | The so-what gives the argument a reach beyond its own case. | Every town has a street that could be a room on Sundays. |

### 2.5 Research Paper (IMRaD) (The Academy) · five steps · no deal question; the Abstract is dealt last with its question saying so

| # | Title | Question | "?" in one sentence | Example *(invented: houseplants and attention in classrooms)* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Introduction | What is known, what isn't, and what question does this paper answer? | The introduction moves from what the field knows to the gap this paper fills. | Studies show plants calm offices; none has tested a classroom during a timed task. |
| 2 | Methods | What did you do, exactly, so someone else could repeat it? | Methods is the procedure in enough detail to be reproduced. | Two classes, one with twelve plants and one without, the same twenty-minute attention test, four weeks. |
| 3 | Results | What did you find, before you say what it means? | Results reports the findings as data, with no interpretation. | The plant class scored eleven per cent higher in weeks three and four; weeks one and two were level. |
| 4 | Discussion | What do the results mean, what are their limits, and what comes next? | Discussion interprets the results, names what they cannot show and points to the next study. | The gap appears only after two weeks, which suggests habit rather than novelty; two classes is a small sample. |
| 5 | Abstract (write me last) | In five sentences, what did you ask, do, find and conclude? | The abstract is the whole paper in a paragraph, written after the rest so it can be true. | One sentence each for the question, the method, the result and the meaning. |

### 2.6 Business Proposal (The Business Desk) · seven steps · no deal question

| # | Title | Question | "?" in one sentence | Example *(invented: a bakery's weekly bread box)* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Problem | What problem does your reader have, in their words? | The problem is the reader's pain, stated the way they would state it. | Regulars say they run out of bread midweek and the shop is closed when they finish work. |
| 2 | Solution | What do you propose, and how does it solve exactly that problem? | The solution is the proposal, matched point for point to the problem. | A paid weekly box of three loaves, left in a locker the customer opens with a code. |
| 3 | Market | Who wants this, how many of them are there, and how do you know? | The market is the evidence that enough people want the solution. | Forty regulars said yes on a counter survey; two hundred live within a mile. |
| 4 | The Ask | What do you need from the reader, exactly? | The ask is the specific decision or resource the proposal requests. | Permission to install two lockers by the door and a loan for the first month's flour. |
| 5 | Timeline | What happens when, from yes to running? | The timeline is the plan in dates, so the reader can picture it happening. | Lockers in two weeks, twenty subscribers in a month, break-even by the third. |
| 6 | Budget | What does it cost, and what does it return? | The budget is the money in and out, honestly, with the return the reader cares about. | Eight hundred for lockers; sixty subscribers cover it in nine weeks. |
| 7 | Objections | What will the reader worry about, and what is your answer to each? | Objections is the reader's doubts, answered before they are raised. | Theft from the lockers: codes expire at midnight, and the first month is insured. |
