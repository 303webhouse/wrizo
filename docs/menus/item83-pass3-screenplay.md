## FIRST PASS

### THE EXPERTS — why the emptiness is the feature

**The form is the toolbar.** A screenplay's format IS its element types; the craft
carries the chrome. Wrizo's wager on this surface — semantic format, keyboard-native —
means the Desk's job is to stay out of the way and hold the few acts that are truly
acts. The null test's pass condition: an open panel that reads as a *cleared desk*, not
a missing one.

**Two kinds of instrument, told apart.** The Clock (SC2's derived pagination) and the
instrument lane under the page are instruments **of the surface** — they live on the
paper and belong to it. The panel's foot holds instruments **of the writer** — goal,
settings, theme. The null test sharpens this line, and SP4 writes it down so
paper-instruments never drift into panels in some later lane.

### THE ARCHITECTS — the mapping

**One section, and it stands.** Screenplay's Desk panel: the **Structure** section
holding the conversion verb — DR3's mirror, **"Convert to Prose…"**, confirm-gated,
destination named — above the shared foot. The committee argued whether a one-row
section earns its engraving; the answer is yes: uniformity over special-casing (G2),
and the heading names the row's kind. The grammar's minimum is *one section + the
foot*, and it must read as a desk. Phase 3 renders it at both widths so the sitting
judges the read, not the argument.

**The absence law goes fractal (SP2).** The typewriter does not run on screenplay
(SC1 S3, `data-typewriter="false"`, pending Nick's own revision). G3 therefore removes
the Typewriter instrument from script's foot — absent, not disabled. And the same law
binds *inside* the gear: the pop-out's typewriter-species rows (Typewriter, Fade
depth) are absent on script, while Progress and Timer ride. Absence at the section
level, the row level, and the pop-out-row level is one law, not three — G3 binds at
every depth. When the SC arc's revision makes the typewriter real on script, the
instrument returns whole, cluster and all, without this lane lifting a finger.

**The strip is the map — a tension between two of Nick's words (SP3).** The strip on
screenplay shows all five ratified strings with Free Write dark ("Draft law only");
prose pages show Revise and Workshop as coming-soon tabs everywhere. Read strictly, G3
and M1 call a dark tab a locked door. But the five strings are **ratified exact** —
Nick's own word — and there is an honest reading that keeps both laws whole: **the
mode strip is the practice's map, not a bank of doors.** A map may show terrain you
cannot enter yet; hiding Revise would misrepresent the practice's shape, and hiding
Free Write on script would misstate the craft rather than the paper. Under this
reading M1 governs the *hands* — controls, menus, capabilities — and the map is exempt
by its ratified nature. The strict alternative (dark tabs absent until real) is named
and stands beside it. Two of his words, one tension, his lock to resolve.

**The element socket stays SC's (SP5).** Chamber B suggests Desk-side element
cycling and scene navigation — a real case exists (tablet hands lack easy Ctrl+1–8,
and the house is laptop/tablet-first). The grammar's whole contribution: *if* item 62
and Nick ever build it, an **Elements** section mounts above Structure as glyph rows,

### THE OPPOSITION — named chair: Marketing / simplicity

**Objection 1 — the null test proves nothing.** A surface with one control fits any
grammar; calling it a test flatters the design. **Answer:** the test is not whether
one control *fits* — it's whether the frame around one control reads as intentional at
both reference widths without padding. That is exactly what empty products fail. The
mockups answer it, which is why SP1 ships to Phase 3 as a rendered pair, not prose.

**Objection 2 — the map reading is a rationalization.** SP3 dresses a locked door in
cartography. **Answer, given weight:** the strict reading is preserved as a full
alternative, and the tension is honestly between two ratified words — the five exact
strings and M1 — not between the committee and a law. The chair's suspicion is why
SP3 goes to the lock sheet instead of becoming a candidate ruling with a winner.

**Objection 3 — flagging "Copy script text" invites churn.** It works; writers use
it; naming it risks losing it. **Answer:** naming it risks nothing — the two
resolutions both keep script copy possible (the house's own anti-slop stance protects
copy-*out*). What naming prevents is a ratified law quietly rotting on one surface.

### THE COGNITION / ADHD BENCH

The one-section desk must carry **zero apology** — no placeholder text, no "more
coming," no explanatory furniture (the null test's bench form). The conversion verb
names its destination (DR3's bench law holds). Keyboard-nativeness is the bench's
*preferred* state on this surface — pointer paths, if SC ever adds them, must never
tax the keys. And the strip's dark tabs, whichever way SP3 resolves, must be visually
quiet: a dark tab that draws the eye is the worst of both readings.
