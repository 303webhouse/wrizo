# ITEM 165 · PRESET DAY-ONE CONTENTS — what each preset arrives with
### Authored 2026-09-22 by the item-84 desk (TUTOR) · approved presets, contents for Nick
**STATUS: CONTENTS FOR NICK'S WORD.** The preset *names* are approved — Nick's word,
2026-09-22: **"Approve"** — and the trademark flag is ruled **"Yes"**, so the beat sheet
ships under a generic name. What follows is each preset's **day-one contents**: the cards
it arrives with, one prompt line each.

**THE GOVERNING CONSTRAINT, verbatim from item 165's amendment:** *the menu's job is
**getting a writer creating fast, not organizing**.* Every preset below therefore arrives
**populated**. A preset that produces empty lanes has produced a naming chore, and a
writer who must name eight cards before writing a sentence has been organized, not
started.

**Why each card carries exactly one prompt line.** Not a description of the beat — a
**question the writer can answer in prose**. The difference is the whole design: *"Midpoint
— the story's middle turn"* explains a term; *"What do they learn here that changes the
goal?"* can be answered by writing. Prompts are written to be answerable, and to be
**deletable** — the first act inside a card is to replace the prompt with the writing.

## §1 · STORY STRUCTURE → STORYTELLING

### 1 · THREE-ACT — three lanes, five cards

The common spine, the one every other structure below is a refinement of. Chosen as slot
one because it is the least presumptuous: five cards, and the writer sees the whole shape
without scrolling.

| Lane | Card | Prompt line |
|---|---|---|
| Act I | **Hook** | What is the first thing that makes this world feel off? |
| Act I | **Inciting Incident** | What happens that they cannot walk away from? |
| Act II | **Midpoint** | What do they learn here that changes the goal? |
| Act II | **Crisis** | What is the worst version of the thing they feared? |
| Act III | **Resolution** | What is different about them on the last page? |

### 2 · THE FIFTEEN BEATS — fifteen cards, in order

**On the name.** This is the widely-taught fifteen-beat commercial structure. Its *shape*
is not ownable and is taught everywhere; the best-known *name* for it is a trademark, and
Nick's ruling is that the generic name ships. The beat names below are this desk's own
wording for the same shape, chosen to be plain rather than clever.

| # | Card | Prompt line |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Opening Image** | What does this world look like before anything changes? |
| 2 | **The Setup** | What is the life that is about to end? |
| 3 | **The Statement** | What does this story argue — said once, early, by someone who does not know it matters? |
| 4 | **The Disruption** | What arrives uninvited? |
| 5 | **The Refusal** | Why do they not go? |
| 6 | **The Break** | What do they leave behind to enter the new world? |
| 7 | **The Second Line** | Who carries the argument the hero cannot? |
| 8 | **The Play** | What is fun about this, before it is hard? |
| 9 | **The Midpoint** | What raises the stakes and takes away the easy exit? |
| 10 | **The Closing In** | What starts going wrong that they cannot fix? |
| 11 | **The Loss** | What is taken that they believed was safe? |
| 12 | **The Dark Night** | What do they believe about themselves here? |
| 13 | **The Turn** | What do they finally understand? |
| 14 | **The Finale** | What do they do that they could not have done on page one? |
| 15 | **Closing Image** | What does this world look like now? |

### 3 · THE HERO'S JOURNEY — twelve cards, in order

Serves the Worldbuilder and the whole quest tradition directly. Twelve stages, no lanes —
the journey is one sequence and lanes would misrepresent it.

| # | Card | Prompt line |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The Ordinary World** | What is normal here, and what is already wrong with it? |
| 2 | **The Call** | What asks them to leave? |
| 3 | **The Refusal** | What are they afraid to lose? |
| 4 | **The Mentor** | Who has done this before? |
| 5 | **Crossing the Threshold** | What is the first thing that cannot be undone? |
| 6 | **Tests and Allies** | Who proves useful, and who proves not what they seemed? |
| 7 | **The Approach** | What do they prepare for that they do not yet understand? |
| 8 | **The Ordeal** | What does it cost? |
| 9 | **The Reward** | What do they carry out? |
| 10 | **The Road Back** | What follows them home? |
| 11 | **The Return** | What is the final test, and what does it test? |
| 12 | **The New World** | What can they do now that they could not before? |

## §2 · STORY STRUCTURE → SCREENWRITING

**What makes these three different in kind from the storytelling three:** the page number
*is* the structure. A screenwriter's beat is a position in a runtime, so **every card
below carries its page target in its own text**, and the prompt comes second. This is the
one branch where the numbers are content rather than guidance.

### 1 · FEATURE — THREE-ACT WITH PAGE GATES — three lanes, eight cards

| Lane | Card | Prompt line |
|---|---|---|
| Act I | **Opening** | pp.1–10 — what establishes the tone before anything happens? |
| Act I | **Inciting Incident** | ~p.12 — what starts the story? |
| Act I | **Act One Break** | ~pp.25–30 — what decision locks them in? |
| Act II | **Midpoint** | ~p.55 — what reverses? |
| Act II | **Low Point** | ~p.75 — what is lost? |
| Act II | **Act Two Break** | ~pp.85–90 — what is the last choice available? |
| Act III | **Climax** | ~pp.95–105 — what is the confrontation? |
| Act III | **Last Image** | to ~p.110 — what is the last thing the audience sees? |

### 2 · THE SEQUENCE METHOD — eight cards

The standard film-school spine: eight sequences of roughly twelve to fifteen pages, each
a small complete movement with its own tension.

| # | Card | Prompt line |
|---|---|---|
| A | **Sequence A** | pp.1–15 — the status quo, and the problem that breaks it. |
| B | **Sequence B** | pp.15–30 — the first attempt, and the lock-in. |
| C | **Sequence C** | pp.30–45 — the new world's rules, learned the hard way. |
| D | **Sequence D** | pp.45–60 — the plan, and what it costs to try it. |
| E | **Sequence E** | pp.60–75 — the reversal: what was true is not. |
| F | **Sequence F** | pp.75–90 — everything collapses. |
| G | **Sequence G** | pp.90–105 — the last plan, made from what is left. |
| H | **Sequence H** | pp.105–end — the confrontation, and the after. |

### 3 · TV PILOT — COLD OPEN AND ACTS — six cards, one optional

Television act structure is genuinely different, not a variation on the feature. The
pilot carries two jobs at once — this week's story and the series' own question — and the
prompts name both.

| Card | Prompt line |
|---|---|
| **Cold Open** | The teaser before the titles: what makes them stay? |
| **Act One** | This week's problem — and the series' question underneath it. |
| **Act Two** | The complication that makes the easy answer wrong. |
| **Act Three** | The cost — and what it reveals about someone. |
| **Act Four** | This week resolved; the series' question left open. |
| **Act Five** *(where the format takes five)* | The last turn. |
| **Tag** | The short scene after: what did this week change? |

## §3 · OUTLINE

### 1 · TRADITIONAL — a numbered hierarchy, three top-level headings

Numbered as the academics require (1, 1.1, 1.1.2), expandable at any level. Three
headings and no more: enough to show the shape of the instrument, few enough that the
writer is not filling in a form.

| # | Card | Prompt line |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **[First section]** | What does the reader need first? |
| 2 | **[Second section]** | What does the first section make possible? |
| 3 | **[Third section]** | What does all of it add up to? |

*The bracketed titles are placeholders the writer overwrites — the numbering is the
preset's contribution, not the names.*

### 2 · MIND MAP — one centre, four children

| Card | Prompt line |
|---|---|
| **[The subject]** *(centre)* | Name the thing in the middle, in as few words as you can. |
| **·** ×4 *(radiating)* | What is attached to this? |

Four unnamed children rather than four prompts: a mind map's whole value is that the
branches are not pre-decided, and four is enough to show that branching is what this
board does.

### 3 · FROM MY PAGES — the reverse outline

**Its day-one contents are the writer's own pages, and that is the point.** This is the
only preset on the list that arrives carrying nothing of its own: the writer selects
pages, and the board arrives as their cards, in the selection's order, each card named by
its page's title. **No prompt cards** — a prompt would be this desk talking over work the
writer has already done.

**ROUTED TO PLAN DESK, per Fable:** *"From My Pages goes to PLAN DESK to design against
the spine."* The contents above are craft and are settled; **what is not settled is
whether the resulting board BECOMES the manuscript's order or merely SHOWS it.** That is
the spine question, and the preset promises different things depending on the answer:

- If the board **becomes** the order, this preset is the Pantser's answer to *"find the
  book"* — he arranges cards and the manuscript follows.
- If the board only **shows** the order, it is a reading of the pile rather than a
  reordering of it — still useful, materially less.

**This desk does not choose**, and flags that the preset's *name* should not imply the
stronger reading until PLAN DESK settles it.

## §5 · TWO THINGS THAT APPLY TO EVERY PRESET

**1 · The prompt is the first thing deleted, and should be built to be.** A card's prompt
occupies the card's body, not a tooltip or a placeholder attribute, so that the writer's
first keystroke replaces it. A prompt that survives alongside the writing becomes
furniture; a prompt that must be hunted and cleared is worse than none.

**2 · No preset writes prose, and none of these prompts are prose.** Every line above is
a **question**, and that is deliberate under the arc's standing law: the Tutor never
supplies the writer's words, and a preset is not a loophole around it. A preset that
arrived with an example sentence in a card would have shipped composition through the
back door of a menu.
